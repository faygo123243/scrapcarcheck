Scrap Car vs Selling Privately — Which Pays More?

The honest comparison: return, effort, risk and time to completion for each route.

When a car reaches the end of its useful life, two options come up most often: scrap it or sell it privately. The right answer depends entirely on the car's condition, age and how much of your time you want to spend on it. Here is the full picture.

When scrapping makes more sense

Scrapping is almost always the better choice when the car meets any of these criteria:

  • No current MOT and the cost of repairs to pass would exceed the car's value
  • Non-runner or requires a tow to move
  • Accident damage, fire damage or flood damage
  • Over 15 years old and worth less than £500 on the open market
  • Missing major components (catalytic converter, wheels, battery)
  • You want it gone within a week with no viewings

Key point: A car in genuinely poor condition that might fetch £400 privately — after weeks of waiting and multiple viewings — will often fetch £200 to £300 as scrap with zero effort. The gap narrows quickly once you factor in your time.

When selling privately makes more sense

Private sale is worth pursuing when:

  • The car is roadworthy with a valid MOT
  • It is less than ten years old and has a private sale value above £1,000
  • It is a sought-after make or model (low mileage, popular colour, full service history)
  • You are prepared to wait several weeks for the right buyer
  • You can safely arrange viewings and test drives

The hidden costs of selling privately

Private sale headline prices look good until you account for the full picture. Advertising on AutoTrader for a car under £1,000 costs around £30 for a basic listing. You may need to pay for a valet (£50 to £150) to make the car presentable. Part-worn tyre checks, bulb replacements, and minor repairs to justify the asking price can quickly add another £100 to £200. Viewings that do not convert take hours. Buyers who do not show up are common.

When you subtract these costs and divide by time spent, the effective hourly rate on a private sale for a car worth under £800 is often very poor.

What about part-exchange?

Part-exchanging at a dealer is quicker than a private sale but typically pays the least of all three options. Dealers build in a profit margin, and for high-mileage, older or damaged cars they will often offer below scrap value. Always get a scrap quote first so you know your floor price before walking into a forecourt.

A real-world example

Consider a 2010 Ford Focus with 110,000 miles, no MOT, and a failing head gasket. A private buyer paying £500 is unlikely because the repair bill to fix it could reach £1,200. A dealer would offer £150 to £200 at best. As scrap, a Focus of that age and weight in full working condition (complete vehicle, all parts present) would fetch around £180 to £250 depending on current steel prices. For a car that cannot be sold, scrap is the only realistic route and the return is comparable to a private sale to a trader — without the hassle.

Frequently asked questions

Can I get more by stripping parts before scrapping?

Rarely. ATFs price the car as a complete unit. Removing the catalytic converter, alloy wheels or battery before calling the scrap yard almost always reduces your offer by more than the parts fetch individually on eBay. The exception is rare performance parts on desirable vehicles — if you know the specific part has a strong resale market, it may be worth removing before scrapping the shell.

What if my car is stolen or scrapped without my consent?

Licensed ATFs are required by law to verify ownership before accepting a vehicle. They must record the identity of the person presenting the car. This is why using regulated ATFs via a comparison service matters — illegitimate scrap dealers have fewer safeguards.

Is scrapping a car bad for the environment?

The opposite. Licensed ATFs drain all fluids and recover hazardous materials safely. Around 80% of a car by weight is recycled — steel, aluminium, copper wiring and glass. The CoD exists precisely to confirm the car has been disposed of in an environmentally controlled way.
